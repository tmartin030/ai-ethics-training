The Case

United States v. Heppner, No. 25-cr-00503-JSR (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 10, 2026; written opinion Feb. 17, 2026)

Judge Jed S. Rakoff, Southern District of New York

First federal ruling squarely addressing whether consumer AI chat logs are protected by attorney-client privilege or work-product doctrine.

What Happened

  • Bradley Heppner, a Dallas financial services executive, was arrested in November 2025 and charged with securities fraud and wire fraud (related to Beneficient and GWG Holdings Inc.).
  • After receiving a grand jury subpoena and retaining counsel at Quinn Emanuel, Heppner independently used Anthropic's consumer Claude AI to research legal issues related to the government's investigation.
  • He fed information he had learned from his attorneys into Claude, generating 31 documents containing prompts and responses that outlined defense strategy and potential legal arguments.
  • He shared these AI-generated documents with his legal team.
  • The FBI later seized these materials during a search of his residence.
  • The government sought to use the documents. Heppner's counsel moved to suppress, claiming attorney-client privilege and work-product protection.

What the Court Held

1. Claude is not an attorney

"Heppner d[id] not, and indeed could not, maintain that Claude is an attorney." — Judge Rakoff

Attorney-client privilege requires a communication between a client and a lawyer (or the lawyer's agent) for the purpose of obtaining legal advice. Claude is none of these. Claude's own materials state it chooses responses that "least give impression of giving specific legal advice."

2. No reasonable expectation of confidentiality

"[There was] simply no reasonable expectation of confidentiality." — Judge Rakoff

Anthropic's consumer terms expressly permit disclosure to "governmental regulatory authorities" and allow use of prompts and outputs for model training. The court held that submitting information to a system with these provisions destroyed any expectation of confidentiality.

This is the holding that matters most for attorneys. The court didn't just say "Claude isn't a lawyer" (obvious). It said that consumer Terms of Service destroy confidentiality — and that applies to anyone using consumer AI, whether client or attorney.

3. Not work product

Sharing information with a consumer AI tool "effected a waiver by destroying the confidentiality that work product protection presupposes." — Judge Rakoff

The work-product doctrine protects materials prepared in anticipation of litigation by or at the direction of counsel. Heppner created the documents on his own initiative. Neither Heppner nor Claude are legal counsel. The documents did not reflect the mental impressions or strategy of Heppner's actual attorneys.

4. Sharing unprivileged documents with a lawyer doesn't make them privileged

Pre-existing documents that were never privileged in the first place don't gain privilege protection just because a client sends them to a lawyer. The AI-generated reports were unprivileged from the moment Heppner created them by submitting information to a consumer AI tool.

The Kovel Carve-Out

"[H]ad counsel directed Heppner to use Claude, Claude might arguably be said to have functioned in a manner akin to a highly trained professional who may act as a lawyer's agent within the protection of the attorney-client privilege." — Judge Rakoff

This is an important signal. The court left open the possibility that AI tools used at counsel's direction, as part of the legal representation, might receive privilege protection under a Kovel-type arrangement. But that's not what happened here — Heppner acted on his own.

For attorneys: This is the distinction that matters. If you direct your client to use a specific AI tool as part of your representation, there may be a privilege argument. If your client uses consumer AI on their own and hands you the results, there isn't one. Either way, the consumer ToS problem remains — which is why the tier of the tool matters regardless.

Why This Matters for Attorneys

Whose chats were these?

A client's. Not an attorney's. This is an important distinction. The case did not hold that an attorney's own AI use waives privilege. But the court's reasoning about consumer ToS destroying confidentiality applies to everyone — client and attorney alike.

What Heppner actually held

A client's independent use of consumer AI, without counsel's direction, does not create privileged or work-product-protected materials — even if shared with counsel afterward.

What the reasoning implies for attorneys

If consumer ToS destroy a client's expectation of confidentiality, they destroy yours too. The privilege analysis turns on the same consumer terms whether you're the client or the lawyer.

Three things to do now

  • Tell your clients. Advise clients that anything they type into consumer AI (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) is potentially discoverable. Add this to engagement letters.
  • Use the right tier. If you use AI for client work, use Tier 3 (API with DPA) or higher. The court explicitly distinguished enterprise/API tiers with contractual confidentiality protections.
  • Check your tools. Consumer accounts with training "turned off" (Tier 2) may be better than Tier 1, but the court's reasoning focused on the ToS provisions about disclosure and data use — not just training. A DPA (Tier 3) is the floor.

How This Maps to the Tier Framework

Tier 1-2 (Consumer)

ToS permit disclosure to government, use for training, and unilateral changes. Heppner found "simply no reasonable expectation of confidentiality" under these terms.

Tier 3-4 (API / Enterprise)

DPA creates bilateral contract. Vendor is data processor, not controller. Contractual confidentiality protections. The court explicitly distinguished these tiers.
