  • ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024): Informed consent triggered by "self-learning" tools that train on input.

Missouri What Missouri Requires (Informal Opinion 2024-11)

Missouri's ethics opinion sits in the moderate middle of the national spectrum. It does not require blanket AI disclosure or firm-wide policies. But it does require specific vendor vetting:

Lawyers should assess "the terms and conditions of using a generative AI platform or service to understand the security of the information being inputted, how that information is being used by the platform or service, and what data sources the platform or service is using."

That's three things to check before you use any AI tool with client work:

  • Security of inputs — what happens to the data you type in? (See Tier 1-4 framework below)
  • How inputs are used — does the platform train on your data? (See vendor cards below)
  • Data sources — what is the platform trained on? (Matters for hallucination risk)

This matrix is built to help you answer all three questions for every major tool. ABA Formal Opinion 512 goes further and applies as persuasive authority — treat it as the floor, not the ceiling.

* "No routine human review" means no humans read your data to improve or train the AI product. It does NOT mean no human can ever access your data. Every vendor — including enterprise and ZDR tiers — retains carve-outs for: (1) legal process (court orders, subpoenas, law enforcement requests), (2) safety and abuse detection (CSAM, terrorism, threats of violence — mandatory under federal law, e.g. 18 U.S.C. § 2258A), (3) terms of service / acceptable use policy violations flagged by automated systems, and (4) security incident investigation. "No human review" is about whether your data is systematically read by reviewers to improve the product. At enterprise/API/ZDR tiers, the answer is no. But no vendor can promise absolute zero human access under all circumstances.

Advertising & Data Selling

Training is not the only risk. Some vendors use your AI conversations to target ads — and the opt-out mechanisms are not what you'd expect.

Key finding: On Microsoft Copilot Free, opting out of model training does NOT opt you out of advertising use. Microsoft's privacy policy states: "This setting will not exclude your conversations from being used for … advertising."

"Are my AI conversations showing up in my ads?"

  • Meta AI: Yes, by design. This is stated policy.
  • Microsoft Copilot Free: Yes, by design.
  • ChatGPT Free/Go: Yes, within ChatGPT itself (not across the web).
  • Google Gemini: Officially no, but your Gemini usage occurs within the broader Google ecosystem where Search, YouTube, Gmail, Maps, and Chrome activity already build your ad profile. Even if chat content is walled off, Google knows you used Gemini, what features you used, and when.
  • Everything else: More likely explained by existing tracking — browser cookies, social media pixels, search history, and the Baader-Meinhof effect (you notice ads for things you're already thinking about).

Feedback Is Training Data

When you click thumbs up or thumbs down on an AI response, you are giving the vendor that entire conversation for training.

Every major AI vendor treats user feedback as a training signal. When you rate a response, flag an error, or submit a bug report, the vendor retains that interaction — including your prompt and the AI's response — for model improvement. This is true even if you have opted out of general training.

  • OpenAI: Thumbs up/down feedback causes that conversation to be retained for training, even in "Temporary Chat" mode and even with training toggled off. Source (CTRL+F: "thumbs up or thumbs down")
  • Anthropic: User-submitted feedback retained for up to 5 years. These exceptions are not opt-outable — turning off the training toggle does not prevent feedback from being used.
  • Google: Thumbs up/down on Gemini responses are retained as feedback data, separate from the general activity toggle.

Rule: Never provide feedback (thumbs up/down, ratings, bug reports) on any conversation that contains client information. The feedback button is a consent button.

Legal Process & Notification

If a prosecutor, grand jury, or civil litigant subpoenas your AI chat logs from the provider, will the provider tell you in time to assert privilege and move to quash? ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) says you must confirm this before using a tool with client data: lawyers must "ensure that the lawyer will be notified in the event of a breach or service of process."

United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 2026): Judge Rakoff held that documents generated through consumer-tier Claude AI were not protected by attorney-client privilege or work-product doctrine. The court found Anthropic's consumer terms provided "simply no reasonable expectation of confidentiality" and that sharing information with a consumer AI tool "effected a waiver by destroying the confidentiality that work product protection presupposes." The court indicated the result "might differ if the AI use had been directed by counsel under a Kovel-type arrangement." Enterprise/API tiers with contractual confidentiality protections were explicitly distinguished.

OpenAI
  • Notification: Yes — "to the extent legally permitted."
  • Where: Law Enforcement Policy v.2025-12 (effective Jan 1, 2026); DPA — search for "reasonable efforts to notify"
  • Exceptions: Gag orders, NSLs, FISA, emergencies (danger of death or serious physical injury)
  • Consumer vs. API: Consumer data subject to standard retention and legal process. Enterprise/API: ZDR available; enterprise data was carved out of the NYT v. OpenAI 20-million-log production order (S.D.N.Y. Nov. 2025)
  • Transparency report: Semi-annual
Anthropic
  • Notification: Yes. Strongest posture — for API/enterprise customers, Anthropic "will generally ask the requester to contact our customer in the first instance" (redirects law enforcement to you before producing data)
  • Where: Privacy Center — Government Requests Policy — search for "contact our customer"; Law Enforcement Requests
  • Exceptions: Gag orders, child safety, emergencies
  • Consumer vs. API: Consumer: direct notification, but Heppner found no reasonable expectation of confidentiality under consumer terms. API/Enterprise: LE redirected to customer first
  • Transparency report: Transparency Hub (launched Feb 2025)
Google
  • Notification: Yes, by email where permitted
  • Where: How Google Handles Government Requests; Cloud DPA — search for "promptly notify Customer"
  • Exceptions: Emergencies, legal prohibition
  • Consumer vs. Workspace: Consumer: standard notification. Workspace: Access Transparency, Access Management, and Access Approvals cover Gemini data (since Oct 2025). Google tries to redirect enterprise requests to the customer directly
  • Transparency report: Extensive, long-running; separate enterprise cloud reporting
Microsoft
  • Notification: Yes. Most aggressive challenge posture — publicly commits to "challenge every government request for commercial and public sector customer data where it can lawfully do so." Redirected 62% of enterprise requests in reported data (107 of 172 in H1 2023)
  • Where: Data Law — Our Practices — search for "prior notice" and "challenge every government request"
  • Exceptions: Secrecy orders (31% of U.S. demands in H1 2025 — 1,974 orders), emergencies, child exploitation
  • Consumer vs. Enterprise: Both get notification where permitted. Enterprise: stronger contractual commitments and active redirection of LE to customer. M365 Copilot data inherits tenant security policies, Purview integration for sensitivity labels and legal hold
  • Transparency report: Semi-annual, detailed
Perplexity
  • Notification: Enterprise DPA only — "to the extent permitted by law." No consumer notification commitment found
  • Where: DPA; Enterprise ToS
  • Exceptions: "To the extent permitted by law" (not further enumerated)
  • Transparency report: None published
Clio
  • Notification: Yes — "prompt written notice (to the extent permitted by law) prior to such disclosure so that the Subscriber may seek a protective order." Also applies a minimum-necessary standard: "furnish that portion (and only that portion) of the Confidential Information that it is legally compelled or otherwise legally required to disclose."
  • Where: Clio ToS — search for "prompt written notice" and "protective order"; DPA
  • Exceptions: "To the extent permitted by law"
  • Transparency report: None published (publishes Legal Trends Report but not government data request statistics)

Bottom line: No vendor can guarantee notification when subject to gag orders, NSLs, or FISA orders. The strongest technical protection is zero data retention — if data is never stored, there is nothing to produce. The strongest contractual protection is a DPA with explicit pre-disclosure notification and an enterprise agreement where the vendor redirects law enforcement to you first. Consumer ToS provide the weakest protections and, per Heppner, may not support any expectation of confidentiality at all.

Ethics Opinions & Guidance on AI

Every formal and informal ethics opinion, guidance document, and court policy addressing lawyer use of AI that we could find as of February 2026. Organized by jurisdiction. The landscape is changing fast — use the tracker links at the bottom to stay current.

ABA (American Bar Association)
  • Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) — First comprehensive AI ethics opinion. Duties of competence, confidentiality, communication, candor, supervision, and reasonable fees all apply. Must verify outputs for hallucinations. Must obtain informed consent before inputting client data into AI tools that train on input.
Alaska
  • Ethics Opinion 2025-1 (2025) — AI use ethically permissible but lawyers must understand the technology, review all outputs, and anonymize prompts when using non-closed AI systems.
California
  • COPRAC Practical Guidance (Nov 2023) — First state-level AI guidance. Never input confidential client info without adequate protections. Do not rely on AI outputs as substitute for independent analysis.
District of Columbia
  • Ethics Opinion 388 (April 2024) — Must understand technology sufficiently to be reasonably confident it advances client interests. Must verify outputs. Must protect confidentiality especially with free tools that exploit inputs.
Florida
  • Advisory Opinion 24-1 (Jan 2024) — May use AI but must protect confidentiality, develop oversight policies, avoid improper billing (no double-billing), and ensure potential clients know when communicating with a chatbot. [PDF]
Kentucky
  • Ethics Opinion E-457 (March 2024) — Notable: failing to use an available AI tool may constitute a competence failure. Fees must be reduced when AI reduces time. Cannot charge clients for general AI training costs.
Michigan
  • Advisory Opinion JI-155 (2023) — Ethical obligation under MRPC 1.1 to maintain technological competence including understanding AI's benefits and risks.
Mississippi
  • Formal Ethics Opinion 267 (Nov 2024) — Must safeguard confidential info, verify accuracy, use reasonable billing, obtain informed consent for AI use. [PDF]
Your Jurisdiction Missouri
  • Informal Opinion 2024-11 (April 2024) — Advisory Committee of the Supreme Court of Missouri, Office of Legal Ethics Counsel. Key requirements:
  • Vendor vetting: Lawyers must assess "the terms and conditions of using a generative AI platform or service to understand the security of the information being inputted, how that information is being used by the platform or service, and what data sources the platform or service is using."
  • Competence: Must educate themselves on generative AI types and understand risks and benefits
  • Confidentiality: Must make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure (Rule 1.6(c))
  • Candor: Must ensure accuracy of AI-assisted work product submitted to tribunals
  • Disclosure: AI use disclosure not required as blanket rule; assessed case-by-case
  • Supervision: Must supervise nonlawyer use of AI
New Jersey
  • Supreme Court Preliminary Guidelines (Jan 2024) — AI must be employed with the same commitment to diligence, confidentiality, and honesty as traditional methods. Must verify all AI information because AI can hallucinate.
New Mexico
  • Formal Ethics Advisory Opinion 2024-004 (Sept 2024) — Must understand AI tool parameters, refrain from inputting confidential info without protections, verify all citations, charge only actual time, develop firm-wide AI policies.
New York (NYC Bar)
  • Formal Opinion 2024-5 (Aug 2024) — Guardrails, not hard restrictions. Must review all outputs for accuracy. Must not input confidential info into "open" AI without client consent. Addresses 12+ rules of professional conduct. [PDF]
  • Formal Opinion 2025-6 (Dec 2025) — Must obtain client consent before using AI to record calls. Must verify transcript accuracy. Must include retainer provisions barring client recording without notice. [PDF]
New York (NYSBA)
  • Task Force Report & Recommendations (April 2024) — Adopted by House of Delegates. Recommends AI guidelines: understand risks/benefits, notify clients, protect confidential data, don't let AI replace professional judgment.
  • Report on AI and Access to Justice (Jan 2026) — Client-facing AI requires strong governance, transparent grounding, clear disclaimers, escalation procedures. Recommended enterprise-grade ZDR, RAG grounding, mandatory human review.
North Carolina
  • Formal Ethics Opinion 2024-1 (2024) — Must maintain technological competence regarding AI. Must evaluate vendor security protocols. Must verify all AI-generated filings. May need informed consent before using AI on client matters.
Oregon
  • Formal Opinion 2025-205 (2025) — Must conduct due diligence on AI tools, undertake adequate training, verify every citation and factual statement, may not inflate billable hours, must establish firm-wide AI policies.
Pennsylvania
  • Joint Formal Opinion 2024-200 (May 2024) — Joint opinion (PA Bar + Philadelphia Bar). AI does not replace personal review. Must be transparent with clients, colleagues, and courts. Addresses competence, confidentiality, conflicts, candor, supervision, and UPL.
Texas
  • Ethics Opinion 705 (Feb 2025) — Must understand how AI functions. Must not disclose confidential info via inputs. Must independently verify all outputs. Cannot bill for hours not genuinely worked, but may pass through reasonable AI subscription costs with client agreement.
Virginia
  • Legal Ethics Opinion 1901 (Nov 2025) — No per se requirement to reduce fees solely because AI reduced time. No per se requirement to disclose AI use unless extenuating circumstances. Must still verify citations and supervise AI use by others.

The through-lines (every opinion agrees on these):

  • Competence: You must understand the AI tool before using it with client work
  • Confidentiality: Client data must be protected — the question is how much diligence the opinion demands
  • Verification: You must personally verify every AI output before relying on it or submitting it to a court — no exceptions anywhere
  • Supervision: Supervising lawyers are responsible for ensuring ethical AI use by staff
  • AI is not your lawyer: The tool does not exercise professional judgment and cannot replace yours

The fault lines (where opinions diverge):

  • Client disclosure: Virginia and Kentucky say no unless extenuating; ABA and North Carolina say yes when data is at risk; NYC Bar says always for chatbots and recording
  • Fee reduction: Kentucky says you must reduce; Virginia says you don't have to; everyone else is in between
  • Vendor vetting: ABA and Missouri demand specific evaluation of vendor terms and notification practices; Virginia and Michigan are silent
  • Firm policies: Oregon and New Mexico mandate written policies; most states are silent on this
  • Affirmative duty to use AI: Kentucky stands alone in suggesting that not using available AI could be a competence failure

For Missouri criminal defense attorneys: Missouri Opinion 2024-11 sits in the moderate middle. It requires you to educate yourself and assess your AI platform's data handling, but does not mandate blanket disclosure or firm-wide policies. However, ABA Opinion 512 applies as persuasive authority everywhere — its vendor-vetting and informed-consent requirements represent the direction the profession is heading. When in doubt, follow ABA 512 as the floor.

Key Terms

Training
The process of feeding data into an AI model to change its future behavior. When a vendor says it "trains on your data," it means your prompts and the AI's responses are incorporated into the model's weights — permanently. Once trained, your data cannot be extracted back out, but it has influenced the model. Training is the core ethical concern: it means your words become part of the product other people use. Not the same as the AI remembering your conversation within a single session — that's inference.
Inference
When the AI reads your prompt and generates a response — the actual "thinking" step. Inference is temporary by nature: the model processes your input, produces output, and (at the API tier) discards both. Inference does not change the model. Every time you type a question and get an answer, that's inference. The ethical question is not whether inference happens — it always does — but what the provider does with your data after inference.
Prompts
What you type into the AI tool — your questions, instructions, and any text you paste or upload. In the legal context, prompts are the primary vector for confidentiality risk. A prompt containing a client's name, case number, or unique facts is identifiable data the moment you hit send. Prompts are sometimes called "inputs."
Responses
What the AI generates back to you — the answers, drafts, analysis, or summaries it produces from your prompts. Responses can also contain sensitive information because they reflect what you told the model. Some vendors retain responses alongside prompts; others discard both. Also called "outputs" or "completions."
Content
An umbrella term vendors use in their ToS to mean prompts + responses + uploaded files + feedback — everything flowing between you and the AI. When a vendor's terms say "we may use your Content to improve our services," that means all of it. Read carefully: some vendors define "Content" to include things you might not expect (e.g., metadata, usage patterns, device information).
Personal Data
Any information that identifies or could identify a specific person. In criminal defense: client names, case numbers, dates of birth, SSNs, addresses, victim names, witness names, officer names, unique fact patterns, and anything in a police report, confession, or piece of discovery. For GDPR and most DPAs, "personal data" has a specific legal definition that triggers data protection obligations.
DPA vs. ToS
A Data Processing Agreement (DPA) is a bilateral contract that legally binds the provider to specific data protection obligations — what they can do with your data, how long they keep it, what happens if there's a breach, and your rights as the data controller. Terms of Service (ToS) are the provider's rules — they set them, they can change them unilaterally, and your only recourse is to stop using the product. API access typically comes with a DPA automatically. Consumer products run on ToS. This distinction is the most important concept in the entire matrix.
Data Processor vs. Data Controller
A data processor handles your data on your behalf, following your instructions. It has no independent right to decide what to do with your data. API providers are processors — they process your prompts, return results, and that's it. A data controller decides the purposes and means of processing. Consumer AI products where the company sets training defaults, retains data for its own use, and changes terms unilaterally are acting as controllers. You want your AI provider to be a processor, not a controller, because a processor is legally accountable to you.
Data Handler
A general, non-legal term for any entity that touches your data at any point in the pipeline. In a tool like Clio Duo, there are multiple data handlers: Clio (the vendor), Azure (the cloud host), OpenAI (the model provider via Azure). Each handler's data practices matter. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link. When evaluating a tool, ask: who are all the data handlers, and what are each of their commitments?
Zero Data Retention (ZDR)
The provider deletes your data immediately after inference — nothing is stored, even temporarily beyond the processing window. The gold standard, but no bar opinion currently requires it. Enterprise-tier products (Harvey, Azure GCC High) typically offer ZDR. Some vendors use "ZDR" loosely to mean "short retention" (e.g., 30 days for abuse monitoring) — read the actual terms.
Legal Process Notification
Whether the AI vendor will tell you before handing your data over in response to a subpoena, court order, or law enforcement request. This matters because attorney-client privilege and work product must be actively asserted — if you don't know about the request, you can't move to quash or seek a protective order, and your privileged data gets produced unopposed. ABA Opinion 512 requires lawyers to confirm notification commitments before using a tool with client data. The strongest vendors (Anthropic API, Microsoft enterprise) redirect law enforcement to the customer first. The weakest (consumer tiers, Perplexity consumer) make no specific notification commitment. No vendor can guarantee notification when subject to gag orders or national security letters.
"No Human Review"
When a vendor says "no human review," they mean no humans routinely read your data to evaluate quality or improve the AI product. This is an important protection — at consumer tiers, human reviewers (including third-party contractors) read a sample of conversations to rate the AI's responses, and those reviewed conversations may be retained for years. But "no human review" is not an absolute guarantee that no human can ever see your data. Every vendor retains carve-outs for: legal process (court orders, subpoenas), safety and abuse detection (CSAM reporting is mandatory under 18 U.S.C. § 2258A), terms of service violations flagged by automated systems, and security incident investigation. The correct question is not "can a human ever see this?" but "is my data being systematically read to improve someone else's product?"
Re-identification Risk
Even "de-identified" data can reveal a client's identity. You are a named user with a finite client list. Case.net is public. Context accumulates across a conversation — a single vague question is safe, but a 50-message thread about the same case with specific facts, dates, and charges can become identifiable even without names. Rule of thumb: if a stranger reading your prompts could figure out who the client is, treat it as identifiable.
