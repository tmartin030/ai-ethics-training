Missouri Your Jurisdiction — Informal Opinion 2024-11

Advisory Committee of the Supreme Court of Missouri, Office of Legal Ethics Counsel. Missouri sits in the moderate middle of the national spectrum. It does not require blanket AI disclosure or firm-wide policies, but it does require specific vendor vetting.

Key requirements:

  • Vendor vetting: Lawyers must assess “the terms and conditions of using a generative AI platform or service to understand the security of the information being inputted, how that information is being used by the platform or service, and what data sources the platform or service is using.”
  • Competence: Must educate themselves on generative AI types and understand risks and benefits
  • Confidentiality: Must make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized disclosure (Rule 1.6(c))
  • Candor: Must ensure accuracy of AI-assisted work product submitted to tribunals
  • Disclosure: AI use disclosure not required as blanket rule; assessed case-by-case
  • Supervision: Must supervise nonlawyer use of AI

Through-Lines: Where Every Opinion Agrees

  • Competence: You must understand AI before using it. General awareness is not enough — you need to know how the specific tool works, what it gets wrong, and where it hallucinates.
  • Confidentiality: Client data must be protected. Every jurisdiction agrees that entering confidential information into an AI tool without understanding data handling is an ethics violation.
  • Verification: You must personally verify every output. No jurisdiction allows a lawyer to submit AI-generated work without independent review of citations, facts, and reasoning.
  • Supervision: Supervising lawyers are responsible for AI-assisted work by anyone they supervise — associates, paralegals, staff. Rules 5.1 and 5.3 apply.
  • AI is not your lawyer: AI cannot replace professional judgment. The lawyer remains the decision-maker and bears full responsibility for the work product.

Fault Lines: Where Opinions Diverge

  • Disclosure: Virginia and Kentucky say no general disclosure requirement. ABA and NYC Bar say informed consent is needed. Most jurisdictions fall somewhere in between.
  • Fee reduction: Kentucky says you must reduce fees when AI saves time. Virginia says no per se reduction is required. Most are silent.
  • Vendor vetting: ABA and Missouri demand specific evaluation of AI tool terms, data handling, and security. Virginia and Michigan are silent on the issue.
  • Firm policies: Oregon and New Mexico mandate written firm-wide AI policies. Most jurisdictions — including Missouri — are silent.
  • Affirmative duty to use AI: Kentucky stands alone in suggesting that failing to use AI could itself be a competence failure. No other jurisdiction has gone this far.
